## what is grammatical?
Understanding those previous rules helps us understand sentences better.

A _grammatical_ utterance is any _possible_ utterance in a language. 
An _ungrammatical_ utterance is, therefore, _impossible_ (A native / proficient speaker of that language would never produce that ungrammatical sentence naturally)

A sentence with double negatives ("Aleemah don’t see nothing") may be ungrammatical / inappropriate to some speakers, but not all.
- This sentence uses <span class="definition">Double Negatives</span>

Also, a speaker has the context of real-world usage and appropriateness. For instance, they'll know when to use _familiarity_ and _formality_.  (What y’all watchin'? `vs` What are all of you watching?)
- This change in "style" is an example of <span class="definition">register</span> (The first sentence is an informal register, the second one is formal)
- Register is not about formality, but *appropriateness*. You don't just go "Might I trouble you to wipe the table, if you don't mind?" to a friend. That would be weird

There are two approaches to grammar: Prescriptive Grammar, and Descriptive Grammar
- Prescriptive like "Prescribe" like "Here is what you should do"

<span class="definition">Prescriptive Grammar</span> /  Traditional Grammar: Rules / statements by a language authority recorded in grammar books and textbooks. It recommends how a language is spoken
- Prescriptive grammarians believe in an absolute standard of correctness
- This approach to grammar is what educated speakers or other individuals with authority use

In prescriptive grammar:
1. One form is more logical than another.
2. There is an appeal to classical forms.
3. There is a preference for older forms of the language.
4. There is an injunction against the use of foreign words (words borrowed from other languages)

According to this view, language is either _correct_ or _incorrect_. Prescriptive rules are viewed positively and utterances that don't follow the rules are viewed negatively.

>[!example] Here are some examples
>Don’t end a sentence with a preposition
>- At what are you looking? <span class="emphasis">not</span> What are you looking _at_?
>- To whom are you talking? <span class="emphasis">not</span> *Who are you talking _to_?
>
>Don’t split an infinitive (to + base form)
>- To understand gradually the facts. <span class="emphasis">not</span> To _gradually_ understand the facts
>- He seems to like it. <span class="emphasis">not</span> He seems to _really_ like it
> 
> Don’t use double negatives
>- I saw somebody. <span class="emphasis">not</span> I didn’t see nobody
>- I have something. <span class="emphasis">not</span> I haven’t got nothing 
>

Note how the "prescriptive grammar" might be objectively worse to read. That's because it does not care about "natural speech" or "speaker preferences". It functions only to assign a binary judgment


## 2.2

<span class="definition">Descriptive Grammar</span> Rules of grammar based on how people *actually* speak. Utterances are not better / worse than other ones (No "correct" form)

Descriptive rules / statements describe how people speak a language in its entirety.
Linguists (generally) consider themselves descriptive grammarians.

When linguists tackle a grammar of a language, they attempt to describe all aspects of the rules that speakers use to speak that language. #not-confident (What is this even for)

Because descriptive statements aim to record speakers’ mental grammar, as a speaker you probably wouldn’t be able to consciously describe them!

Descriptive linguists counter the prescriptivist claims with the following:

1. All varieties of a language are valid systems with their own logic and conventions.
2. There is no scientific reason to expect one language to match the mold of another.
3. Languages are continually changing in subtle ways without reducing their usefulness, preciseness, or aesthetic value. 
4. All languages have adopted words from other sources.

What's the difference between the rules of Descriptive Grammar and Prescriptive Grammar?
- Prescriptive rules are about mandating language
	 - They tell us what speakers _should_ and _shouldn’t do_ 
 - Descriptive rules are about describing language
	 - They tell us what speakers _do_ and _don’t do_

<span class="definition">Word order</span> The position of the subject, verb, and object (In English it's SVO)

There can be overlap with Prescriptive and Descriptive language. For instance, if you speak in a <span class="definition">Language Variety</span> that is more "prestigious", then you may follow both prescriptive and descriptive rules

You can also follow *certain* rules. For instance, in Standard Canadian English, double negation is a no-go (which is a rule of prescriptive grammar) however, negation with a ‘no-’ word (nothing, no one, nowhere) would be considered prescriptively ungrammatical.

<span class="definition">Standard Language</span> Spoken by the most powerful group of a community. Usually based on the speech of the rich, powerful, and highly educated.
- Prescriptive Grammarians consider this "correct" or "ideal"

>Non-standard language does not mean ungrammatical language!
>- Just as an utterance can be informal, a sentence can be non-standard and still be grammatical

### Characteristics of Grammar 

Linguists also refer to mental grammar as <span class="emphasis">linguistic competence</span>. <span class="emphasis">Linguistics performance</span>, on the other hand, is the actual language used in any given situation

To explain why we need to distinguish between prescriptive and descriptive approaches to grammar, let us first learn five simple characteristics of grammar
1. <span class="emphasis">Generality</span>: All languages and dialects have a grammar
	- If a language can be spoken, it has a system of rules governing it!
	- However, individual grammatical systems differ in the use of structure formation rules
		- `The two dogs now see several kangaroos` can be written as `See now kangaroos several dogs two` in Warlpiri.
2. <span class="emphasis">Parity</span>: All grammars are equal. 
	- There is no such thing as a “good grammar” or “bad grammar”. All grammars perform the same function, which is to (unconsciously) instruct people on how to form and interpret words and sentences of their language to successfully communicate with others
3. <span class="emphasis">Mutability</span>: Grammar can change
	1. New words are added all the time.
	2. Language never "peak" (bro, English in the 1700's was peak!)
4. <span class="emphasis">Inaccessibility</span>: Grammatical knowledge is unconscious / intangible 
	- Our knowledge of language systems is described as mental grammar
	- Because language rules cannot always be figured out just by thinking about them, we consider linguistic knowledge to be inaccessible or subconscious.
		- We just *know* that sentences sound right / wrong. That is subconscious
5. <span class="emphasis">Universality</span>: All grammars are alike in basic ways
	1. All languages share certain principles and properties.
	2. For example: All languages use a small set of contrastive sounds to help distinguish words from each other. For instance, the ‘s’ and ‘z’ sounds in English are contrastive because we can use them to create words with distinct meanings: ‘sap’ and ‘zap’.

 Note how language's structure can be far different from English, or purely spoken. Regardless, they are not "grammar-less". They all have structure.
