What is a free morpheme? What is a bound morpheme?
?
Free: A morpheme that can stand by itself (be its own word)
- Happy, left, etc. are examples
Bound: Requires a root morpheme.

What type of syntactic category are roots in?
?
The lexical categories (nouns, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, etc.)


Complex words have roots and bases
- Bases are what affixes are attached to.
- A root is affixed to either become a word or a base
- A base is affixed to either become another base or a word
- Roots are bases, but not all bases are roots
- "The number of bases in a word will always match the number of affixes"
	- Look at unbelievable. There are two affixes and two bases (believe and believable)
- The core meaning comes from the root (which may not always be freestanding!)
- Bases are "forms" that an affix is affixed onto.

## Review seminar 1

Morphology
- simple and complex words
- morphemes
- There are 3 processes when morphemes are attached to each other (morpheme + morpheme). Two of them can involve affixation (derivation and inflection)
	- Derivation
		- Affix changes the meaning or category of the form
		- Happy (adj) + ness = happiness (noun)
		- un + faithful (adj) --> unfaithful (adj) but the meaning changes (it is the opposite!)
			- faith = believe. unfaith = NOT believe. Totally different! 
		- Acronyms (Derivation)
			- NASA, UNICEF, ROM (read as a word)
		- Abbreviation (initialism) (Derivation)
			- Read letter by letter
		- Eponymy (Derivation)
			- Words coming from proper names (like peoples names or places names)
		- Blending (kinda like compounding) (Derivation)
			- When words A and B are parts of a new word. 
			- Unlike compounding, with blending we do not keep the entirety of the individual free forms.
			- Brunch is an example. Smog. Motel.
		- Clipping (Derivation)
			- Words that are made from omitting syllables in existing words 
			- Influenzas --> flu
			- Mathematics --> math
			- Lingustics --> lin
		- Backformation
			- Backformation = removal of syllables / affixes
			- Words like 'editor' which came from french had their suffix removed to make the verb "to edit". 
			- Exam will have the og word and the new word, so just say "derivation; backformation"
		- Zero derivation / Conversion
			- Changing the syntactic category without doing anything to the word (Google -- noun and verb)
			- `Ink` used to be a noun but is now also used as a verb
			- Sleep (N) and Sleep (V) is zero derivation!
	- Inflection
		- Affix does not change the meaning or category, but adds some kind of information
		- No change in category; no change in meaning, just grammatical information
		- In the lecture she went over them all. <span class="emphasis">All the inflectional affixes are suffixes</span> in English. Derivational ones could be suffixes or prefixes
		-  If you know all 8 of the inflectional affixes, the rest of them are derivational!  <span class="emphasis">WARNING</span>. There are cases where these prefixes are also derivational (strength --> strength`en`) so make sure you pay attention to the context and structure.  
			- Nouns:
				 -  `'s` 
					 - The manager's report. 's only shows possession
				  - `-s plural`
					- The Managers
			  - Verbs
				  - `-ed` (past tense)
				  - `-ing` (progressive / in progress) (teach --> teaching)
				  - `-s 3rd person singular` (3rd person singular) (walk --> he walks)
				  - `-en` (past participle; completion of action) (eat --> eaten)
			  - adj/adv
				  - `-er`
					  - Tall vs taller (comparison)
				  - `-est`
					  - Tall vs tallest 
		  - Ways to do it:
		  - Affixation (suffixation or prefixation)
			  - We already went over it above
		  - Internal change (flipping a vowel or two)
			  - Substituting non-morphemic segments (like vowels) to mark a change in grammar
			  - Sing (present) --> sang (past)
			  - Run (present) --> Ran (past)
			  - Drive (present) --> Drove (past)
			  - <span class="emphasis">Ablaut</span> is specific (vowel change to changes grammatical info)
				  - Usually for verbs and past/present tense
			  - <span class="emphasis">Umlaut</span> is another (mouse --> mice)
				  - Usually for nouns and plurality. Again, vowels change
		  - Suppletion (Only for verbs) (total change)
			  - Eat + ed = ate (it's not regular at all!)
			  - `good – better – best` is a better example imo
			  - A total replacement of a morpheme with another
			  - `Think + ed = thought ` is another example
		  - Reduplication
			  - Repetition of a word
			  - Partial 
				  - When a bit of a word is repeated 
			  - Total
				  - When a word is repeated COMPLETELY
			  - "home" might be a dorm but "home home" is your parents home.  That's total reduplication. 
	- Compounding
		- Combining two words (free forms)
		- Cup + board = cupboard
		- spoon + feed = spoon-feed (verb)
		- Usually the element on the far right decides what category the word is (in spoon + feed, feed is a verb, so spoon-feed is a verb)
			- That is the "head" of the compound
		- Between `green house` and `greenhouse`, the stress is on the rightmost element `house` for non-compounds. Stress on the leftmost means it's a compound 
			- ![[Pasted image 20241207201550.png]]
		- There is `endocentric` and `exocentric` compounds
			- endocentric = the meaning comes from (usually) the head and it makes sense. Easy to predict. dogfood is food for a dog! sky-blue is a type of blue! caveman is a type of man!
			- exocentric = meaning is NOT predictable
				- redhead = NOT a type of head!
				- Greenhouse = NOT a 'green' house! It's a house for plans

- With both derivation and inflection, it has to do with adding affixes 
	- Suffixes (most popular in english)
	- Prefixes (most popular in english)
	- inflexes in some languages
	- circumfixes
- You will need to specify suffixation, prefixation, etc. when any composition process occurs

	- un + adj it means "not" that thing.

All the Articulatory (phonetic) processes
- Assimilation (nearby sounds become similar)
	- Liquid devoicing (circle under the liquid)
		- If there's a liquid in a stressed syllable before a voiceless stop (p, t, k), the liquid will become devoiced.
		- If after a voiceless stop (p, t, k) there is a liquids or glide, it will become devoiced
		- l and r, j and w
	- (vowel) Nasalization
		- When a vowel comes before a nasal consonant (n, m, ŋ)
		- regressive assimilation (right affects left)
			- The sound that doesn't change is what is "affecting" 
	- Place Assimilation
		- The place of your tongue blends with the word after.
		- in these examples, `in` is meant to negate:
			- `in`tolerable
			- `iŋ`credible
			- `im`possible
		- as you can see, the sounds are regressively affected!
- Dissimilation
	- Making sounds *less* similar for emphasis or clarity (fifths)
- Deletion
	- Removing sounds 
- Epenthesis
	- Adding sounds that shouldn't exist at a phonemic level
- Metathesis
	- Swapping around sounds
- Tappling/Flapping
	- when you add that curvy r instead of /t/ or /d/.
	- Happens when /t/ or /d/ is between two vowels where the first vowel is stressed 
- Aspiration / voice lagging (adding the h)
	- `'p, 't, 'k` followed by a vowel. Have to have stress
	- Aspiration affects voiceless stops at the beginning of a _stressed syllable_ while the voiceless stop is immediately followed by a vowel
- Canadian Raising (using a caret instead of a)
	- Where Diphthongs /aw/ or /aj/ are raised (rise vs rice is the best example of this) 
	- ai or aw + voiceless consonant (so not a vowel) (so p, t, k)
- Syllabic consonant
	- shwa + nasal / liquid = shwa gets deleted.  
		- l and r are liquids / approximants
	- This is relevant to phonology as now the liquid/nasal is a syllabic
