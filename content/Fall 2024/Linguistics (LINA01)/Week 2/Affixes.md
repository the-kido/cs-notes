- Are morphemes
- In total, there are 4 types of affixes (suffixes, prefixes, infixes, and circumfixes)
- When affixes attach to other morphemes, it is <span class="definition">Affixation</span>

<span class="definition">Infixs</span> are unlike affixes in that they break apart morphemes and wiggle their way into the center of one. 
- `Abso-freaking-lutely` is an example of that. `Absolutely` was the morpheme that was split up; `-freaking-` is the infix. 
- Currently, English only allows <span class="emphasis">expletive infixation</span> (a morpheme inserted into another morpheme for emphasis; usually uses a profane word). 
- In other languages like Inuktitut, they can be used to form new verbs using `-palia-`, 

<span class="definition">Circumfixes</span> surround a morpheme. There are none in English, but there are in German.
- Unlike a separate prefix and suffix, these are *together*
_**ge**__-lieb-__**t**_ means "have loved" where `lieb` means love and `ge- -t` is a circumfix that makes a verb past tense. 

### <span class="definition">Roots</span>
- Another morpheme. 
- Contains the core meaning of a word
- Complex words are made of a root and affix(es)
- They can either be free or bound by nature
	- Receive, perceive, and deceive have a <span class="definition">bound root</span> `-cieve`, since it doesn't mean anything by itself
	- In Prehistoric, `history` is a <span class="definition">free root</span>.


### Qualities of affixes
1. Affixes attach to very specific kinds of bases. 
	-  -able applies to verbs and not nouns or adjectives.
	- -ish attaches to nouns and some adjectives, but never to verbs. 
1. Derivational affixes can change the syntactic category of a word they attach to
2. Words ending with -_able_ become adjectives while words ending with -_ment_ or -_ness_ are nouns.  

### Bases
- A morpheme or a group of morphemes that an affix can attach 
- In some cases, the base is just the root.
	- In `kindness`, the affix `-ness` attaches to the base `kind`, which is also the root
	- In `joblessness`, we..
		1. Attach `-less` to the first base (which is the root `job`)
		2. Attach `-ness` to the second base `jobless`
	1. In `unhappiness`...
		1. Happy is the base
		2. We could add "ness" to the end and get `happiness` as a base
		3. However, `happiness` is a noun; you can't add un- to a noun. Unchair??
		4. So, instead the first base is `unhappy` and we add -ness after that since unhappy is an adjective (red-ness, sad-ness, etc. are fine!)
1. The number of bases in a word will always match the number of affixes

### Derivation and Inflection
A <span class="definition">Derivational Affix</span> attaches to a morpheme / word and changes its meaning and/or change its syntactic category.
- Using affixes to change the meaning of a word
- There is <span class="emphasis">derivational suffixation</span> and <span class="emphasis">derivational prefixation</span>
- Some examples are `-er`, `-ment`, `-al`, and `-ize`.
- For instance, condition (noun) becomes conditional (adjective) when adding `-al`
- Usually, these are created as a separate "entry into our lexicon"

In cases where it only changes the meaning, adding `un-` does exactly that.
- Lawful --> Unlawful (the opposite meaning!)
- Stop --> Nonstop 

<span class="definition">Inflectional Affixation</span>
- Add grammatical information to a word 
	- This could be tense, number, etc.
- Do not change the syntactic category 
- Attach last
