<span class="definition">Morphology</span> "The study of the systems of rules which form our knowledge of word structures"
- It looks at what governs the formation of words
It literally means "the study of form". 
- Morpho- (meaning "form", "shape") and "-logy" (meaning "science", "study of"). 

<span class="definition">Lexicon</span> Our mental dictionary of words

<span class="definition">Mental Dictionary</span> Stores information about words and the rules used to build them

#### Signs
The building blocks of any communication system are called <span class="definition">signs</span>.
Signs can be <span class="emphasis">iconic</span> or <span class="emphasis">non-iconic</span>
- The relationship between the <span class="emphasis">form</span> (the shape or sound of something) and its meaning is either obvious (iconic) or arbitrary (non-iconic)
	- Onomatopoeia are iconic. Otherwise, most words are non-iconic.
	- The way I see it; if the form is a literal symbol of the thing it's describing, it's iconic. Otherwise, it's non-iconic.

## What is a word?
A word is..
- an element whose position is _flexible_ relative to neighboring units
- (in linguistics) is the smallest free form unit.
	- A free form can stand on its own

Words made of 1 morpheme are <span class="emphasis">simple</span> words. Words made of more than 1 morphemes are <span class="emphasis">complex</span> words
- `Dis-trust` is complex. `Garden` is simple
- Note that the definition of "complex" means "consisting of many different and connected parts." and not just "convoluted; hard to understand"
- `Lunchbox` is also a complex word because it can be broken down into "Lunch" and "Box", both of which are morphemes (*and* simple words) because they cannot be broken down!

## Allomorphs
- Variations of a morpheme depending on the surrounded environment, but retains the same meaning.
	- That variation can be pronunciation between morphemes
		- for `-ed`, it is pronounced differently in booked; begged; stated

### Word classes
- We normally learn nouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, articles, and interjections.
- In linguistics, we split it up into
	- noun
	- verb
	- adjective
	- adverb
	- preposition
	- auxiliary verb
	- determiner
	- degree word
Word classes can be split into content words and function words

##### <span class="definition">Content words</span> 
- Are nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and prepositions
- These can be "looked up in a dictionary"
 - They are <span class="definition">Open Class</span> because they accept new additions to their categories (except for prepositions...)
	 - `doomscrolling`, mogging, or crypto are new words, and they're mostly nouns and verbs!
 ##### <span class="definition">Function Words</span>
 - Are determiners, auxiliary verbs, and degree words
 - They do not have *lexical* meanings; they just help form an utterance. 
 - `a` or `an` are determiners. You can't really look these up in a dictionary, so they don't have a lexical meaning.
	 - In `pass me a book`, it's' hard to say exactly what the meaning of the word `a` is
 - They are <span class="definition">Closed Class</span> because you cannot add new ones. 


>[!note]  There are languages that express concepts differently from English. Where English refers to certain concepts using free morphemes, other languages use bound morphemes (and vice versa).
