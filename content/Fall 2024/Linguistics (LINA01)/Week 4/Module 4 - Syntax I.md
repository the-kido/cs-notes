<span class="definition">Syntax</span> Rules that describe how words are organized into phrases, and phrases into sentences.
- It's the part of grammar that studies the *structure* of phrases and sentences

## Structure
- Some languages have stricter rules about their word order than others. English is one such language. 
- Italian, on the other hand, has a word order that is considered "<span class="emphasis">free</span>" in comparison to English (i.e., this language allows words to be scrambled, however, Italian still has structure!).

Position of a word in a sentence can change its syntactic category. 



# Identifying lexical categories
## Nouns
- It's not always a person, place, thing, or idea. (e.g. love and kick are nouns)
- There are suffixes like `-ment, -tion, -ity, -ify, and -ness` which form nouns (since they are derivational suffixes)
- Nouns can also carry inflectional suffixes like `-s` to make it plural
> Plural `-s` for nouns is not the same as third-person singular `-s` for verbs 
> - Banana`s` = Noun
> - She walk`s` = Verb
> They are similar-looking but have different functions

So, how do we figure out if a noun is a noun? We figure it out via the attached affixes 
(morphological distribution/evidence) or by syntactic context in which the word appears (syntactic distribution/evidence)
### Morphological Distribution / evidence
- Reality has the derivational suffix `-ity` which makes nouns from adjectives.
- So, this is morphological evidence/distribution, which refrs to the type of affix a word is attached to.

For developers, it is a noun for two reasons
1. `-er` is added to verbs to create nouns (teach`er`, driv`er`)
2. The `-s` is added to make the noun plural.

##### <span class="emphasis">Regular / irregular forms</span>
- Usually, a noun that is plural ends with `-s`. But child does not (it s `children` instead, not `childs`). So, `children` is an irregular form that does not follow the regular plural rule.

### Syntactic Distribution / Evidence 

#### 1. Nouns are preceded by a determiner
Nouns typically come after determiners (the, a , these, this, all, some)
- A <span class="definition">determiner</span> is a word that normally precedes a noun to _specify_ or _identify_ it. Idk if pronouns are determiners tho
#### 2.  Nouns are often subjects or objects of the sentence:
- My <span class="emphasis">son</span> plays the guitar.
- That tired <span class="emphasis">student </span>is applying to law school.
- My manager's daily <span class="emphasis">reminders</span> made us angry.
#### 3. Nouns are sometimes modified by adjectives
a. A hungry <span class="emphasis">lion</span>
b. Those happy <span class="emphasis">children</span>
c. Two constructive <span class="emphasis">comments</span> \
#### 4. Nouns can be preceded by a preposition
Prepositions could be `by, from, in, to, during, after, before`, etc.
- by <span class="emphasis">noon</span>
- before the <span class="emphasis">trip</span>
- under the <span class="emphasis">desk</span>

## Verbs
- There are action verbs (obvious) and state verbs (They express perception, mental states or connecting functions)
- They have many forms:
	-  Infinitive, present tense, past tense, present participle, and past participle.
- You can have main verbs (or just *verbs*) and helping verbs (regular auxiliary verbs and modal auxiliaries)
	
## Auxiliary Verbs
- <span class="emphasis">Regular auxiliaries</span>
	- To have, to be, to do
- <span class="emphasis">Modal auxiliaries</span>
	- Caleb *can* eat 
	- Caleb *might* run
Auxiliaries are usually accompanied by a main verb. They are not required (you can just get rid of them)
>[!example]
>- Our neighbors **are** _mowing_ the lawn. (auxiliary = _**are**_, main verb = mowing)  
>- **He** **did** not _get_ any sleep last week. (auxiliary = **did**, main verb = get)

If an auxiliary is not accompanied by a main verb, then the auxiliary *is* the main verb
>[!example]
>- I _had_ lunch at 2 PM.  
>-  The students _did_ their homework.  
>- The guests _are_ in the lounge.

Modal auxiliaries "carry some meaning, unlike regular auxiliaries"
>[!example] The bolded are modal auxiliaries
>- You **must** _stop_ at the red light.  
>    - Must indicates obligationg
>- It **might** _rain_ tomorrow.  
>   - Might shows probability
>- He **should** _see_ a doctor.
>   - Should shows suggestion

### Morphological Distribution / Evidence of Verbs
- There are suffixes (e.g. `-ing`) that by adding them will be derivational suffixes so you can be sure a word is a verb.
### Syntactical Distribution / Evidence of Verbs
1. Verbs are preceded by auxiliary verbs
2. Verbs come after the subject of a sentence
>[!example]
> - **Usman** *bought* a new car.  
> - **Thornetta Davis** *sings* the blues with passion.

This is cuz the subject is usually the do-er of the action
3. Verbs may come after negation
>[!example]
>I did **not** *sleep* well this week

<span class="emphasis">Not</span> is the negation

## Adjectives

- Adjectives can be inflected (changed via inflection) into comparative and superlative forms using the -_er_ and -_est_ suffixes (e.g., _pretty, prettier, prettiest_)
- Suffixes like `-able`, `-ish`, and `-ful` make adjectives
- Syntactically, adjectives themselves are modified by a functional category that we will call _degree words_ (Deg)
	- e.g. _so, too, very, more, quite,_ and _rather_
- Adjectives can appear between determiners and nouns 
	- (a _sunny_ garden, my _childhood_ dream) 
- Adjectives can appear after the verb `to be` (She is _smart_, Bobby is very _friendly_)

## Adverbs
They describe verbs or clauses
 Syntactically, some adverbs can also be modified by degree words. These adverbs are sometimes called adverbs of manner:
- very slowly
- so inconsistently

They will NOT be where adjectives would be (like between determiners and nouns. No way)
Adverbs contribute information about manner, time, or place (and more)

They have the unique property of occuring in a number of different positions (most occur at the beginning, but they can appear around as well)
>[!example]
>- Slowly, I finished my work for the day.
>- I slowly finished my work for the day.
>- I finished my work for the day slowly.

## Prepositions
Syntactically, prepositions typically appear before nouns (or noun phrases)
- There isn't really any morphological evidence
> on/under/over/in/above the chair

Some prepositions can be modified by degree words like right and straight
- Right under his nose
- Straight into the fire
