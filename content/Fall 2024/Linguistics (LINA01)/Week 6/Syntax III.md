		Constituents are hierarchically organized, and we can use PSRs to represent these hierarchical structures.

<span class="definition">Phrase Structure Rules (PSRs)</span> come in the form `XP → (YP) X (ZP)`
- XP represents the name of a constituent (x could be noun, verb, etc. and P stands for phrase still)
- The brackets indicate the elements are optional (YP and ZP are required)
- The X, however, is **required**. It is the "Head" of the phrase

>[!example] [must listen to her lecture]
>'must' and 'listen' are both verbs. Which one is the head!?
>
>'listen' is, since it's the more required one.
## Noun phrases (NP)
> `NP → (Det) (AdjP+) N (PP+)`
- There could be multiple AdjPs and PPs

>[!example] \[the mud on the floor]
>NP → Det N PP
>![[Pasted image 20241019181839.jpg]]

^ The brackets in the PSRs aren't required because those elements are no longer optional (they are in the PSR)

>[!example] ​\[that sweet honey cake] 
>In PSR notation it would be `NP -> Det AdjP AdjP N`

## Verb Phrases (VP)
> VP → (Aux) (AdvP) V (AdjP) (NP+) (AdvP) (PP+) (AdvP)

>[!example] \[often sings songs on the balcony]
>VP → AdvP V NP PP
>- Often (AdvP)
>- Sings (V)
>- Songs (NP)
>- On the balcony (PP)

## Adjective Phrases (AdjP) and Adverb Phrases (AdvP)
> AdjP → (Deg) Adj (PP)

> AdvP → (Deg) Adv

Adjectives and Adverbs modify noun and verb phrases respectively and can stand on their own as phrases

>[!example] \[very slowly]
>AdvP → Deg Adv
>![[Pasted image 20241019184950.jpg]]

>[!example] \[completely stained]
>AdjP → Deg Adj
>![[Pasted image 20241019185051.jpg]]

>[!danger]
> Many adverbs act as degree words (adverbs of degree) and can be easily confused with AdvPs that form their own constituents.
>>[!example] \[an oversized cozy sweater]
>> NP → DET AdjP AdjP N
>
VS 
>>[!example] \[an extremely oversized sweater]
>>NP → DET AdjP AdjP N
>

>Adjectives are sometimes followed by prepositional phrases 
>"Fond of cheese" is an example.
