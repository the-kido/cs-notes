>[!thm] Integration By Parts
>If u = f(x) and v = g(x) are differentiable.
>Then 
>- (for indefinite integrals) $\int u\cdot dv = u\cdot v - \int v \cdot du$
>- (for definite integrals) $\int_a^b u\cdot dv = u\cdot v|_a^b - \int_a^b v \cdot du$

- We replace f(x) and g(x) with these variables just to make it easier to remember.

To use it, we pick/choose 
- u = __
- dv = __
- du = __
- v = __

When to use:
- If the integrand can be written as a product, and we cannot use u-sub
>- Logarithm / inverse trig and cannot use u-sub.
>	- Why? because we want to be able to compute the "ingredients" as it's easy to get the derivative of.

>[!example] Compute $\int \frac{ln(x)}{x^2}dx$
>
>u is normally the first polynomial, unless there is a logarithm or inverse trig (then that takes priority). Everything left over is the $dv$
>Choose $u = ln(x)$ and dv = $x^{-2}\; dx$
>$du = \frac{1}{x}dx$ and $v = -x^{-1}$
>
>Then $\int \frac{ln(x)}{x^2}dx = \ln(x)(-x^{-1}) - \int-x^{-1} \frac{1}{x}dx$
>(this we get from the int by parts formula)
> $= \frac{-ln(x)}{x} + \frac{-1}{x}$ at the end 


>[!example] Find $\int t^2e^t dt$
>$u_1 = t^2$, so $du_1 = e^t dt$
>$du_1 = 2t dx$ and $v_1 = e^t$
>
>Then the above $= t^2e^t - \int e^t 2t\; dt$
>$=t^2e^t - 2 \int te^tdt$
>oh look we can prolly do parts again yay.
>Choose $u_2 = t$, $dv_2 = e^t dt$,
>$du_1 = 1 dt$, $v_2 = e^t$
>
>the above $= t^2e^t - 2(te^t - \int e^t\; dt)$
>$= t^2e^t - 2(te^t - e^t) + C$ 
>boom

>[!example] $\int_0^1 \arctan(x)\; dx$
>An example of why parts helps us integrate inverse trig!
>
>Choose $u = arctan(x)$, $dv = 1dx$
>$du = \frac{1}{x^2 + 1} dx$ and $v = x$
>
>$\int_0^1 \arctan(x)\; dx = \tan^{-1}(x)\cdot x|_0^1 - \int_0^1 \frac{x}{x^2 + 1}dx$
>Now we do a 'w' sub (lol cuz we used u already).
>Let $w = 1 + x^2$. Then $dw = 2x dx \implies \frac{dw}{2} x\; dx$
>(since we're swapping x out, we also need to swap out the upper integration limit).
>So when x = 0, w = 1, and when x = 1, w = 2.
>$x\tan^{-1}x|_0^1  - \int_1^2 \frac{1}{w}\; \frac{dw}{2}$
>$=1\cdot tan^{-1}1  - 0\cdot tan^{-1}(0) - \frac{1}{2} \ln(w)|_1^2$
>$=\frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{1}{2}(ln(2) - ln(1))$
>$=\frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\ln 2}{2}$
