# Sequences: converge or diverge?
to prove convergence we can prove by "definition"
- the $\epsilon N$ defn
- Convergent properties
- A new guy: the bounded monotone convergence theorem (<span class="emphasis">BMCT</span>)

To prove divergence
- Taking advantage of its bad behavior 
	- If it goes to infty, we use M-N proof
	- If oscillation, we use contradiction

>[!defn] A sequence $\set{a_n}$ is 
>1. Bounded if $\set{a_n}$ is bounded above and below
>	-  i.e. $\exists C \in \mathbb R^{\geq 0}$ s.t. $-C \leq a_n\leq C \; \forall n \in \mathbb N$
>1. Monotone (either always increasing or always decreasing) if $\set{a_n}$ is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing

>(strictly increasing means $>$, not just $\geq$)

Quick example zoom zoom!
>[!example] $\set{1 + (-1)^n}$ is bounded by 2 for sure. But it's not monotonic.

>[!example] $\set{e^n}$ is not bounded above. But it's monotonic (strictly increasing).


##### pg. 600!

>[!thm] Bounded Monotone Convergence Theorem (BMCT)
>IF $\set{a_n}$ is bounded *and* $\set{a_n}$ is monotone
>THEN $\set{a_n}$ must converge!
>
>But we can write this as two cases you'll observe below.
>
>(BMCT is not biconditional)


There are two combinations that are quite useful to eliminate some redundancies for test-writing-purposes!

>[!note] Shortcuts
>Specifically, the fact that some upper bound M is approached by an always-increasing function, then *of course* it'll converge! The increasing will plateau, giving us an HA. So really we can say it'll converge if:
>- $\set{a_n}$ is increasing and bounded above
>	- (we don't need to worry about the lower bound here, clearly!)
>- OR
>- $\set{a_n}$ is decreasing and bounded below
