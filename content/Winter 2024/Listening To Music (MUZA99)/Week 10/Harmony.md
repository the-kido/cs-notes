# Harmony things

<span class="testable">Tonality</span>
-  The organization of music around a single pitch (the <span class="emphasis">tonic</span>)

<span class="testable">Tonic Note</span>
- The central pitch that all other notes gravitate towards. 
- The dominant note is the 5th degree of the scale made by the tonic
- Leading tone is the seventh degree of the scale made by the tonic
	- Y'know that song i always wanted to make? In the downbeat, I use the leading note!
![[Pasted image 20250415095922.png]]


<span class="testable">Key Signature</span>
- The sharps and flats at the beginning that defines the principal notes

<span class="testable">Consonance</span>
- A combination of notes which are in harmony w/ each other
	- Primary
	- Stable
	- Pleasant
- <span class="testable">Dissonance</span>: a lack of harmony among notes
	- Secondary / unstable / unpleasant 
- <span class="testable">Atonality</span>: The absence of tonality
	- <span class="emphasis">Sprechstimme</span>
	- almost impossible to attain without your music sounding abysmal (subjectively)
- Arnold *Schoenberg* was like, yo, every note has the same strength. No tonic note. Every note is the tonic note. You gotta use every note before repeating a note.
	- <span class="emphasis">dodecaphonic</span>


<span class="testable">Chord</span>: Simultaneous sounding of two or more notes
- <span class="testable">Triad</span>: Three-note chords; these are very common chords!
	- Made with intervals of thirds
- Just like scales, they have qualities like major or minor!

> Just to clarify, nothing abt major/minor chords sound happy/sound because of physics. It's all abstract and relationships.
> We talk more about this in the DeNora reading



### Changing chords
- Why change chords? 
	- To find the melody; otherwise the harmony would not support said melody
	- The sequence of chord movement throughout a piece is called a <span class="emphasis">chord progression</span>
![[Pasted image 20250329170850.png]]
- The pace at which chords change is called <span class="testable">harmonic rhythm</span>
	- The rate could be fast/slow and/or regular/irregular
- <span class="testable">Cadence</span>: A harmonic configuration that concludes a phrase
	- <span class="emphasis">Authentic</span> (resolved)
	- <span class="emphasis">Plagal</span> (resolved; "Amen" cadence)
	- <span class="emphasis">Imperfect</span> (unresolved)
	- <span class="emphasis">Deceptive</span> (unexpected / unresolved)
- Chords can change like, once a bar, to once every 8-16 bars. 
	- Hey Jude changes often, but So What changes super rarely. 
	- Rock for instance doesn't really change chords often
	- Sometimes it doesn't change at all!!


# The most complicated concept in this course :)
![[Pasted image 20250329172625.png]]
- A letter followed by some other things is the "root/quality chord"
	- Consider $F^7$ or $G_{MI} \;^{(MA\;7)}$. These are your <span class="emphasis">root/quality chord symbols</span>
- <span class="emphasis">Functional Chord Symbols</span> use the chord's function and quality (e.g. `I` or `ii`)
	- It's a roman numeral that tells us where the chord falls in the hierarchy 
- I (major) <span class="testable">Tonic chord</span> built on the first note of the scale
- ii (minor): The supertonic chord; built on the second note of the scale
- IV: (major): The <span class="testable">subdominant chord</span>
- vi (minor): the submediant chord; build on the sixth note of the scale.

> These numbers are relative to the root (i.e. `I` is the literal root)

> Note for the functional chord symbols that the uppercase is major; lowercase is minor
